// WHAT IS ENCRYPTION?

A field guide to keeping secrets in the open

THE CORE IDEA

Encryption is the process of scrambling information so that only someone with the right key can read it. Think of it like a padlock with a message inside — anyone can see the locked box, but only the person with the key can open it.

Modern encryption uses complex mathematics to convert plain text (readable data) into ciphertext (scrambled data). The transformation is designed to be practically impossible to reverse without the correct key — even with the most powerful computers alive.

On this site, every message you type is encrypted with AES-256 before it travels across the channel. The ciphertext — that wall of hex characters — is what the internet actually sees.

A SHORT HISTORY

~1900 BC
Caesar & Substitution Ciphers
Ancient Egyptians used non-standard hieroglyphs. Julius Caesar shifted letters by a fixed number — "A" becomes "D", "B" becomes "E". Simple, but it worked until someone figured out the pattern.
1467
The Vigenère Cipher
Leon Battista Alberti invented polyalphabetic substitution — using multiple Caesar shifts based on a keyword. Far harder to crack, it was called "le chiffre indéchiffrable" for nearly 300 years.
1940s
Enigma & the Birth of Modern Cryptanalysis
Nazi Germany used the Enigma machine — an electromechanical cipher device with 158 quintillion possible settings. Alan Turing and the team at Bletchley Park cracked it, shortening World War II and laying the foundations of computer science.
1976
Public-Key Cryptography
Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman introduced the revolutionary idea of key pairs — a public key anyone can use to encrypt, and a private key only you can use to decrypt. This solved the oldest problem in cryptography: how do two strangers share a secret in public?
1977
RSA Algorithm
Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman published RSA — an algorithm based on the near-impossibility of factoring very large numbers. Still in widespread use today for secure key exchange.
2001
AES Becomes the Global Standard
The Advanced Encryption Standard, developed by Belgian cryptographers Joan Daemen and Vincent Rijmen, was adopted by the U.S. government. AES-256 is what protects your banking, your messages, and your passwords right now.
Today
End-to-End Encryption & Post-Quantum Cryptography
Apps like Signal use E2E encryption so not even the server can read your messages. Meanwhile, researchers race to build encryption that will survive quantum computers — which could theoretically break today's algorithms.

HOW AES-256 ACTUALLY WORKS

AES (Advanced Encryption Standard) uses a 256-bit key and processes data in 128-bit blocks. It runs the data through 14 rounds of mathematical transformations — substitutions, shifts, mixes, and key additions — until the output looks like pure noise.

PLAINTEXT→hello alice
KEY→3a9f2c1e8b4d7f6a0e5c2b9d...
ROUND 1→9d4f2a8c1b7e3d6f0c5a2e9b...
ROUND 7→f3c8a1e6b2d9f4c0e7a3b5d2...
ROUND 14→a8f3c21b9e4d7a6f2c1e8b5d0f9a...
CIPHERTEXT→a8f3c21b9e4d7a6f2c1e8b5d0f9a3b7c...

Without the key, the ciphertext reveals nothing about the original message. Brute-forcing AES-256 would take longer than the estimated age of the universe, even with every computer on Earth working together.

WHY IT MATTERS

🏦

FINANCIAL SAFETY

Every online payment, bank transfer, and card transaction is encrypted in transit. Without it, your card number is visible to anyone monitoring the network.

🗣️

FREE SPEECH

Journalists, activists, and ordinary citizens in authoritarian countries depend on encrypted communication to speak freely without fear of surveillance.

🏥

MEDICAL PRIVACY

Medical records, prescriptions, and test results are legally required to be encrypted. Your health history is between you and your doctor.

🔐

IDENTITY PROTECTION

Passwords are stored as encrypted hashes. Even if a company's database is stolen, attackers can't read your actual password — only a useless scramble.

THE CHAT ON THIS PAGE

When Alice types a message and hits Send, here's what happens in a real encrypted system:

A shared secret is derived using ECDH (Elliptic Curve Diffie-Hellman) key exchange. That secret is used as the AES-256 key. The message is encrypted, producing ciphertext. An HMAC tag is added to prove the message wasn't tampered with. The ciphertext travels across the network. Bob's app uses the same shared secret to decrypt it, verify the tag, and display the original message.

The smoke animation you see represents the plaintext "disappearing" — once encrypted, the original information is gone from the wire. The falling glyphs on Bob's side represent the ciphertext arriving. The decrypt reveal shows the key unlocking the data.
